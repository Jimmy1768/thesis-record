# Explanatory Axes Design

Status: formation-stage methodology design. No explanatory variable is approved
yet.

## Purpose

Mobility Conversion needs explanatory axes that help investigate why some
Metropolitan Transport Regions produce more usable access than others.

These axes are not outcome dimensions, score components, moral judgments, or
rankings. They are candidate explanations to test against usable-access
outcomes.

## Core Separation Rule

Outcome dimensions describe transport quality and usable access.

Explanatory axes describe candidate conditions that may help explain why usable
access is higher, lower, more resilient, more unequal, or harder to convert.

Do not count an explanatory axis as access performance.

## Candidate Axis 1: Economic Capacity

Working idea: regions with greater fiscal, household, institutional, or capital
capacity may have more ability to build, operate, maintain, subsidize, and
adapt transport systems.

Possible mechanisms:

- public investment capacity;
- operating subsidy capacity;
- household ability to pay;
- institutional staffing and technical capacity;
- maintenance and renewal capacity;
- ability to absorb shocks.

Risks:

- GDP or wealth may be mistaken for transport quality;
- high capacity may mask poor conversion;
- low capacity may still produce strong access through form, governance, or
  lower-cost modes;
- data-rich wealthy regions may appear better because they are more legible.

Failure pressure:

- economic capacity does not explain usable-access differences after boundary,
  urban form, demand, and governance are considered;
- high-capacity regions show weak conversion;
- lower-capacity regions show strong conversion through non-capacity mechanisms.

## Candidate Axis 2: Demand Intensity

Working idea: travel demand pressure can force higher service levels,
coordination, infrastructure use, and political attention, but it can also
overload systems and reduce reliability.

Possible mechanisms:

- density of trips;
- peak demand;
- crowding pressure;
- congestion pressure;
- labor-market integration;
- intensity of non-work travel needs;
- mode competition for scarce street or right-of-way space.

Risks:

- congestion fame may be mistaken for demand intensity;
- high demand may look like failure when it is actually evidence of system use;
- suppressed demand may be invisible where poor access prevents trips;
- demand intensity may be confounded with urban form and economic capacity.

Failure pressure:

- measured demand intensity does not predict service availability, reliability,
  affordability, or access;
- high demand consistently overwhelms conversion capacity;
- low-demand regions produce strong access through different design logic.

## Candidate Axis 3: Urban Form

Working idea: land-use pattern, density, polycentricity, street structure, and
destination distribution shape the difficulty of converting transport resources
into access.

Possible mechanisms:

- proximity between housing and opportunities;
- corridor structure;
- polycentric or monocentric layout;
- street connectivity;
- walking and cycling feasibility;
- transfer burden;
- cost of serving dispersed demand.

Risks:

- density may be treated as destiny;
- beautiful urban form may be mistaken for universal access;
- built-up area boundaries may hide functional travel regions;
- urban form may be confounded with historical wealth, age, and governance.

Failure pressure:

- urban form does not explain access differences once service, affordability,
  and governance are considered;
- dense regions still fail to convert form into access;
- dispersed regions produce usable access through alternative mechanisms.

## Candidate Axis 4: Governance Fit

Working idea: transport performance depends partly on whether decision rights,
funding, land use, operations, fare policy, accountability, and regional travel
patterns fit together.

Possible mechanisms:

- alignment between MTR travel geography and governing institutions;
- regional planning authority;
- fare and service integration;
- funding reliability;
- project delivery capacity;
- accountability for cross-boundary trips;
- coordination across operators and municipalities.

Risks:

- governance fit can become subjective narrative;
- transport authority boundaries may be mistaken for MTR boundaries;
- formal integration may not produce usable access;
- informal coordination may be missed by institution-heavy measures.

Failure pressure:

- coded governance fit does not help explain usable-access outcomes;
- formally integrated governance still converts poorly;
- fragmented governance performs well through other mechanisms;
- coding cannot be made traceable enough to avoid narrative bias.

## Candidate Axis 5: Growth / Maturity

Working idea: rapidly growing, shrinking, mature, newly built, or legacy-heavy
regions face different conversion problems.

Possible mechanisms:

- infrastructure inherited from earlier growth eras;
- lag between population growth and transport capacity;
- deferred maintenance burden;
- greenfield versus retrofit cost;
- growth pressure on affordability and service coverage;
- path dependency in rights-of-way and land use.

Risks:

- maturity may be confused with wealth or urban age;
- fast growth may be treated as failure when the real issue is lag;
- legacy systems may be rewarded for inherited assets without accounting for
  maintenance burden;
- shrinking or slow-growth regions may be excluded despite important lessons.

Failure pressure:

- growth stage does not explain access differences after capacity, demand,
  form, and governance are considered;
- maturity categories are too crude to distinguish mechanisms;
- data availability makes mature formal systems easier to study than fast-growth
  or informal systems.

## Cross-Axis Rules

Each axis must eventually specify:

- candidate measures;
- source classes;
- expected mechanism;
- confounders;
- boundary compatibility;
- missingness risks;
- failure condition.

No axis should be accepted as causal without a design that can distinguish it
from the other axes.

## Interaction Hypotheses

The most important explanations may be interactions, not isolated axes.

Candidate interactions:

- high capacity plus poor governance fit may produce weak conversion;
- high demand plus good urban form may produce strong service economics;
- good urban form plus poor safety may still yield weak usable access;
- fast growth plus fragmented governance may create persistent access lag;
- mature infrastructure plus deferred maintenance may erode inherited access;
- low capacity plus compact form may outperform high capacity plus dispersion.

## Current Formation Position

The five axes should be retained as the initial explanatory frame, but each must
remain provisional until source classes, measures, and failure conditions are
defined.

The strongest version of the thesis will not ask whether rich, dense, famous
regions are good. It will ask which regions convert their conditions into
usable access better than expected, and which convert worse than expected.
