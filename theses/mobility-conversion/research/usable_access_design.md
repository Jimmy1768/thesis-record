# Usable Access Design

Status: formation-stage methodology design. No metric is approved yet.

## Purpose

Mobility Conversion needs an outcome concept that measures transport quality
without collapsing into transit reputation, city reputation, GDP, livability,
or traveler preference for one mode.

The working outcome is usable access performance: the degree to which people in
an MTR can reach opportunities and essential activities through a transport
system that is available, time-competitive, reliable, safe, affordable,
physically usable, and resilient.

## Design Requirements

An acceptable usable-access measure should:

- describe access to activities or opportunities, not transport supply alone;
- include non-car and car-dependent access realities without turning the thesis
  into mode advocacy;
- distinguish service presence from useful service;
- account for distribution, not only regional averages;
- be measurable from traceable sources;
- preserve uncertainty and missing data;
- avoid selecting metrics only because they favor famous transport regions;
- remain separate from explanatory axes such as income, density, governance, or
  growth stage.

## Candidate Outcome Dimensions

### Access To Opportunities

Measures whether people can reach relevant destinations within plausible time,
cost, and physical-access constraints.

Candidate opportunity classes:

- jobs;
- education;
- healthcare;
- groceries and essential retail;
- public services;
- social and civic destinations.

Risks:

- job access can dominate the measure and hide non-work travel;
- opportunity data may be better in wealthy regions;
- destination quantity may not capture quality or suitability.

### Network Coverage And Service Availability

Measures whether transport services and networks are available where people
live and need to travel.

Candidate concepts:

- population near frequent service;
- service span;
- service frequency;
- transfer availability;
- sidewalk and cycling network continuity;
- road-network reach where car travel is structurally necessary.

Risks:

- route presence may overstate actual usefulness;
- coverage can reward sprawling low-frequency service;
- service-area data may not reflect crowding, reliability, or access barriers.

### Travel Time And Reliability

Measures whether trips can be made within reasonable and predictable time.

Candidate concepts:

- median travel time to opportunity classes;
- travel-time variability;
- peak/off-peak difference;
- transfer penalty;
- wait-time burden;
- disruption frequency.

Risks:

- commute-only data may miss essential non-work trips;
- averages can hide severe access gaps;
- reliability data is uneven across modes and countries.

### Transportation Safety

Measures exposure to death, injury, violence, and perceived danger while
traveling.

Candidate concepts:

- traffic fatalities and serious injuries;
- pedestrian and cyclist injury exposure;
- transit safety incidents;
- gendered and age-related safety constraints;
- station, stop, sidewalk, and crossing safety.

Risks:

- police and agency reporting are not comparable everywhere;
- perceived safety may be real but hard to measure consistently;
- fatality rates alone may miss suppressed walking and cycling demand.

### Affordability

Measures whether access is financially usable for households across the income
distribution.

Candidate concepts:

- transport cost as share of household income;
- fare burden for frequent use;
- cost of car dependency;
- access after accounting for housing plus transport cost;
- concession availability and eligibility friction.

Risks:

- fare levels alone are misleading;
- income distribution data may not align with MTR boundaries;
- informal costs, parking, insurance, and time costs may be missing.

### Walking, Cycling, And Universal Access

Measures whether the system works for people outside a narrow able-bodied,
car-owning, peak-commuter model.

Candidate concepts:

- sidewalk continuity and crossing safety;
- protected cycling access;
- step-free station and vehicle access;
- access for disabled people, children, older adults, and caregivers;
- first/last-mile usability.

Risks:

- infrastructure presence can overstate usability;
- accessibility audits are uneven;
- universal access may be reduced to wheelchair access if not defined broadly.

### Asset Condition And Resilience

Measures whether access can be sustained under everyday wear, deferred
maintenance, shocks, weather, and disruption.

Candidate concepts:

- state of good repair;
- service disruptions from infrastructure failures;
- climate and flood exposure;
- redundancy;
- recovery time after disruption.

Risks:

- asset-condition data may be politically sensitive or unavailable;
- resilience claims can become narrative without event data;
- newer systems may look better before lifecycle costs appear.

## Rejection Rules For Proposed Metrics

Reject or quarantine a proposed metric if it:

- measures city reputation rather than access;
- measures GDP, wealth, or investment capacity as the outcome;
- rewards a mode without measuring usefulness;
- depends on a proprietary ranking methodology that cannot be inspected;
- cannot be traced to a source and date;
- cannot be mapped to the MTR boundary or a documented approximation;
- treats missing data as zero performance or high performance;
- duplicates an explanatory axis;
- only works for one country or one institutional model unless the thesis scope
  is explicitly narrowed.

## Current Formation Position

Usable access should start as a multi-dimensional outcome framework, not a
single score.

The first accepted methodology should define each dimension, source standards,
and failure rules before deciding whether any dimensions can later be combined.
